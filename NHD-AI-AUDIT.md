## 5. SIMULATIONS / INTERACTIVE COMPONENTS

### 5.1 Triangle Simulator (`TriangleSimulator.tsx`)
- **Does:** you set baseline `c` and angles `A`, `B`; it solves the triangle and draws it live.
- **Model:** genuine and correct. `C = 180 − A − B`; Law of Sines `a = c·sin A / sin C`, `b = c·sin B / sin C`; area `= ½ab·sin C`; height `= a·sin B`. Angles clamped so `A + B ≤ 178°`.
- **Inputs:** c (1–80 km), A (5°–), B (5°–), mode, preset. **Outputs:** C, a, b, area, height, SVG, narrated steps.
- **Assumptions:** plane (non-spherical) trigonometry — fine at this scale, but the real GTS applied spherical excess corrections, which the site never mentions.
- **PRESETS ARE FABRICATED.** All four — "Madras baseline 1802" (c=12.1, A=78, B=72), "Cape Comorin sight-line 1806" (30/60/70), "One link in the Great Arc 1830" (25/74/68), "Darjeeling → Peak XV 1852" (40/85/88) — are labelled *"Historical presets"* and *"a real Survey scenario"*, but **only the 12.1 km Madras baseline corresponds to a real recorded quantity (7.5 miles).** Every angle is invented. No real triangle from the GTS is reproduced anywhere.
- **The "Cape Comorin sight-line" blurb is also physically wrong:** *"From two hills near Cape Comorin, surveyors sighted Bangalore hundreds of miles north."* A single theodolite sight-line of hundreds of miles was impossible; that distance is exactly what the *chain* of triangles existed to bridge. The blurb contradicts the site's own thesis.
- **Communicates an argument?** Yes — "one measurement, everything else computed." Legend text: *"You measured baseline c and angles A, B / Math computed everything else."*
- **From you:** the concept of a triangulation simulator (from your brief). **From the model:** all math, all code, all presets, all prose.

### 5.2 Everest Calculator (`EverestCalculator.tsx`) — ⚠️ highest-risk component
- **Does:** you set distance, elevation angle, observer altitude, and refraction coefficient `k`; it computes the peak's height with a curvature-and-refraction correction.
- **Model:** `h = h₀ + d·tan θ + (1 − k)·d²/(2R)`, R = 6,371 km. This is the **standard, correct** trigonometric-heighting formula. The math is sound.
- **Inputs:** d (10–250 km), θ (0.05–10°), h₀ (0–5 km), k (0.10–0.20). **Outputs:** uncorrected height, corrected height (m and ft), curvature drop, live diagram.
- **Assumptions:** spherical Earth; single lumped refraction coefficient; sea-level datum; no geoid/plumb-line deflection (which was in fact a real and famous problem for the GTS in the Himalayas — unmentioned).
- 🚩 **THE PRESETS ARE REVERSE-ENGINEERED, NOT HISTORICAL.** They are presented under the heading **"Real observation stations"** with dates: "Jirol station (1849)" d=174 km θ=2.18° h₀=137 m; "Ladnia (1849)"; "Near Darjeeling ridge (1848)". *Jirol* and *Ladnia* are real GTS Himalayan station names — but **the numbers attached to them are not.** The transcript of the original build session contains the line:
>   *"The Jirol preset gives 5,395 m (should give ~29,000 ft). My preset angles were too small;…"*
>
>   **The angles were adjusted until the output matched 29,000 ft.** That is fitting fake inputs to a known answer and labelling the result "real." The README compounds this: *"the Jirol station preset reproduces Peak XV to within 200 ft."* It "reproduces" it because it was tuned to.
- **Also unsupported:** *"Their calibration was good enough to compute Everest to within a few metres of the modern satellite figure."* 29,002 vs 29,032 ft is a 30 ft (~9 m) difference, and the agreement is partly coincidental — the 1852 figure had known systematic errors that partially cancelled. "Within a few metres" overstates it.
- **Communicates an argument?** Yes — that 19th-century trigonometry was near-modern in accuracy.
- **From you:** the idea of an Everest calculator. **From the model:** formula selection, code, presets, all prose, the 29,000 ft back-fit.

### 5.3 Position Fix Simulator (`PositionFix.tsx`)
- **Does:** races an "1850s resection" panel against a "GPS trilateration" panel, with timers.
- **Model:** left = intersection of three noisy bearing lines, fix = centroid of pairwise intersections. Right = intersection of three range circles, fix = centroid of the three intersection points nearest canvas centre. Geometry is real; noise is **deterministic pseudo-noise** (`((i * 37 % 7) - 3) / 3`), not random — the "error" is a fixed pattern, not a simulation of measurement uncertainty.
- 🚩 **THE TIMERS ARE FABRICATED AND THE CODE SAYS SO.** Verbatim comments:
  ```
  setTime1850(Math.round(p1 * 12 * 60))   // pretend the field work took 12 min
  setTimeGPS(Math.round(pg * 350))        // pretend the GPS fix took 350 ms
  ```
  The headline comparison the whole component exists to make — "minutes vs. milliseconds" — is **two invented constants**. The Legacy page instructs the visitor: *"Press Take a fix. Watch the two timers: minutes vs. milliseconds."*
- **Units are fictional too:** the 1850s "fix error" is reported in `"metres"` — in scare quotes in the code — because it is a **canvas-pixel distance**. The GPS panel reports the same pixel distance as `m` with no quotes, implying a real metre.
- **Historical assumptions, all AI-chosen:** that resection-from-three-hills is the right 1850s analogue (the GTS's principal work was *triangulation with theodolites*, not compass resection); "a cheap prismatic compass is good to about 0.5°"; "consumer GPS ±3 m"; "RTK ±1 cm"; "ten thousand times better than a theodolite."
- **Communicates an argument?** **Emphatically yes.** This component *is* the site's legacy thesis rendered as an interactive: same geometry, different speed. That argument, and the numbers that appear to prove it, are AI-generated.

### 5.4 Theodolite Viewer + Eyepiece + Vernier
- **Does:** clickable schematic with 4 labelled parts and a rotation slider; a simulated eyepiece where you align crosshairs on a peak; a vernier scale that reads out D°M′S″.
- **Model:** rotation is cosmetic (pointer angle = slider angle). The eyepiece "lock" threshold is `|error| < 60″`, an arbitrary choice. The vernier decomposes the `aim` value into degrees/minutes/seconds and highlights tick `round(seconds/6)` — a **stylised approximation** of vernier reading, not a real 10-on-9 vernier mechanism (which the glossary correctly describes but the widget does not implement).
- **Assumptions:** the drawing is an **invented schematic**, not a reproduction of Cary's actual instrument. Part descriptions ("36-inch achromatic sighting scope", "divided into single degrees", "five verniers") are unverified model recall.
- **Communicates an argument?** Mildly — that arc-second precision was mechanically achievable.

### 5.5 Survey Map & Timeline Scrubber
- **Station coordinates** (13 stations) are **real modern lat/lng** for real places — the one genuinely accurate dataset in the project (though modern city coordinates ≠ historical station coordinates, and "Peak XV 29,002 ft, computed 1852" carries the contested attribution).
- **Station "year" values** and the **12-triangle mesh in `locations.ts` are invented.** The code comment is candid: the triangles were chosen *"so the network reads as a coherent mesh rather than random overlapping polygons"* — i.e. designed for visual coherence, not copied from a survey chart. The real GTS network had hundreds of triangles in a completely different configuration.
- The map therefore **shows a fictional triangulation network animating over a real map, framed as the historical survey growing.**
- Timeline: 12 events, model-generated, unsourced.
